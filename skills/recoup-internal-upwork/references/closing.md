# Closing: from submitted to signed

Winning the reply is a different skill from winning the bid, and the workspace has
historically stopped at Submitted. This is the other half.

## Reply speed beats everything else here

Answer a client message **within the hour** during working hours, same day
otherwise. Clients messaging a shortlist are usually messaging several people at
once and the ordering of replies shapes who feels like the obvious hire. A
thoughtful answer tomorrow loses to a competent one in twenty minutes.

If a full answer needs research, send the short one now and the long one later:
acknowledge, answer the actual question asked, say when the rest is coming.

## What the first reply has to do

Four things, in about 150 words:

1. **Prove you read their message**, by answering the specific thing they asked
   rather than restating the proposal.
2. **Answer the real question underneath it.** "Have you done X?" usually means
   "will this be a problem?" Answer both.
3. **Propose the next concrete step** with a time attached. A 20-minute call this
   week, or a named first deliverable.
4. **Stop.** No recap of the letter they already read, no enthusiasm padding.

Do not attach anything new unless asked. The proposal already carried the artifact.

## The call is discovery, not demo

The goal is a scoped, paid first step, not a technical performance. Ask:

- **What breaks today?** The concrete symptom, not the roadmap.
- **What have you already tried, and why did it not work?** Reveals both the
  history and how they treat contractors.
- **Who else has to agree?** A founder deciding alone converts in days; a
  committee converts in months or not at all.
- **What is the decision timeline, and what happens if this slips a month?**
  Tests whether the urgency in the posting is real.
- **What is the budget, and is it approved or aspirational?**
- **What does done look like for the first piece of work?**

Talk less than half the time. The client's own description of the failure is
worth more than any answer prepared in advance, and repeating their words back in
the follow-up message is the cheapest trust you can buy.

**Never bill for a first call**, and say so. Thirty minutes unpaid is a cheap
option to sell them and it removes the last reason to hesitate.

## Scope and rate

**Hold the rate, move the scope.** When a client pushes on price, reduce what they
get, never what an hour costs. A discount is permanent, applies to every future
hour, and quietly reclassifies you. A smaller first slice does none of that and
gives them the same lower number to approve.

Useful sentence: *"I would rather do less of it at my rate than all of it at a
discount, and the smallest useful piece is X."*

**Propose a capped first engagement.** A named deliverable, an hour estimate, and
a not-to-exceed number. Caps are what make a stranger comfortable authorizing work
from someone with no history with them, and they cost nothing if the estimate is
honest.

**Hourly with a weekly limit** for open-ended work, so Upwork's payment protection
applies. **Fixed-price with milestones** only when the deliverable is genuinely
fixed, and never with a single milestone at the end.

## The first week

The engagement is being evaluated whether or not anyone says so.

- Ship something inspectable in the first few days, even if small.
- Put verification evidence in every handoff, so nobody has to take your word for
  what an invoice bought.
- Report a problem the day you find it, not the day it becomes unavoidable.
- If the work turns out smaller than scoped, say so and bill less. It is the
  single most effective thing available for winning the second engagement.

## Converting to ongoing work

**Propose the next slice before finishing the current one**, while the value of
the last one is still visible. Name what you found that is out of scope, what it
would cost to address, and what happens if it is left. Then let them decide.

Ask for the review at the moment they are most pleased, which is usually right
after the first thing works, not at the end of the contract.

## Follow-up craft

One week after submitting, then once more, then stop. Two follow-ups is the limit
before it costs more than it can win.

**A follow-up must add information, never request attention.** "Just checking in"
asks the client to do work for you. Instead: something you noticed in their
product, a relevant thing that shipped, a sharper version of the estimate. The
message should be worth reading even if they never hire you.

**Do not follow up on a job with 50+ proposals within the first week.** They have
not started reading. A nudge arriving before the first pass just spends your one
good impression early.

If a proposal is still unviewed after two weeks, it is not a follow-up problem.
It is a targeting or profile problem. See the diagnostic table in `SKILL.md`.

## Platform rules that matter

- **Never move off-platform before a contract exists.** No email, no phone, no
  direct payment. It violates the terms and voids payment protection, which is
  the only thing between you and an unpaid invoice with a stranger.
- **No contact details in attachments.** Cite public work by `repo #number`
  instead of by link.
- **Unpaid test tasks:** a small bounded one is normal. Anything resembling real
  delivery is the job, and the job is paid.

## Walk away from

- Scope growing before a contract is signed.
- A client who will not say what the budget is after being asked twice.
- Pressure to start before terms are written down.
- Anyone asking you to work around the platform's protections in either direction.

Losing one of these is not a loss.
