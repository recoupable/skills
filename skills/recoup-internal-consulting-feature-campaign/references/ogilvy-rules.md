# Ogilvy + Roman & Raphaelson craft rules

The clarity floor for business and persuasion writing: emails, memos, proposals, landing pages, ads, sales copy, stakeholder updates. Ten rules distilled from David Ogilvy's "How to Write" memo (1982) and Kenneth Roman & Joel Raphaelson's *Writing That Works* — the book Ogilvy made every employee read three times.

Use this two ways:
1. **While writing** — keep the rules in mind, especially the short-word swaps and the honesty/no-hedging rule.
2. **As a self-audit before sending** — run the piece against all 10, quote your own offending text, and fix anything a sharp, time-pressed reader would notice. Fix criticals always; fix moderates unless the register argues otherwise.

These stack with anti-slop.md (`anti-slop.md`) (the slop floor) and voice-principles.md (`voice-principles.md`) (the craft ceiling). When a rule collides with the piece, spirit over letter — a 50-word sentence is fine if the rhythm needs it, jargon is fine if the audience lives in it. Break a rule only when breaking it serves the reader.

## Sources

- **David Ogilvy**, "How to Write" memo, September 7, 1982.
- **Kenneth Roman & Joel Raphaelson**, *Writing That Works* (Harper & Row, 1981).

## Rule 1 — Natural voice ("Write the way you talk. Naturally.")

**The test:** Read it aloud. Would a real person say it that way to a respected peer?

**Violation patterns:**
- "Pursuant to our earlier discussion..." (no one talks like this)
- "It has come to my attention that..." / "Please be advised that..." (corporate throat-clearing)
- Passive where active is natural: "A decision was made to..." → "We decided to..."
- Nominalizations: "make a decision" → "decide," "conduct an investigation" → "investigate"

**What natural doesn't mean:** Not casual, not slang. Lawyers and doctors have natural voices that sound like lawyers and doctors. It means the writer sounds like themselves. "LOL" in a board memo is as unnatural as "pursuant."

**Violation:** "It is incumbent upon the undersigned to communicate that the project timeline has experienced a delay."
**Fix:** "I need to tell you the project is running late."

## Rule 2 — Short units ("Use short words, short sentences, and short paragraphs.")

**The tests:**
- **Words:** Is there a shorter word that means the same thing?
- **Sentences:** Past 25–30 words, does it need to be? Can it split?
- **Paragraphs:** Past 5–6 sentences (~100 words), is it one idea or several?

**Ogilvy's short-word swaps:**
- utilize → use
- commence → start, begin
- endeavor → try
- facilitate → help, make easier
- terminate → end, fire
- implement → do, put in place
- demonstrate → show
- approximately → about
- subsequent → next, later
- in order to → to

**Long-sentence diagnostic:** Count clauses. More than two joined by "and / but / which / ;" usually hides two or three sentences. **Paragraph diagnostic:** If you can't point to the one sentence carrying the point, it has more than one point — split it.

**Violation:** "In order to facilitate the implementation of the aforementioned strategy, it will be necessary for all relevant stakeholders to convene at a subsequent date to be determined."
**Fix:** "We need to meet to finalize the plan. I'll send a date."

## Rule 3 — No jargon ("Never use jargon words like reconceptualize, demassification, attitudinally, judgmentally. They are hallmarks of a pretentious ass.")

**The test:** Would someone outside this company or industry know the word — and would a normal word do? If yes to the second, it's jargon.

**Common offenders:** leverage (verb), synergies, verticals, circle back, take offline, bandwidth (metaphorical), drill down, deep dive, low-hanging fruit, ideate, pivot (when not a real pivot), value-add, move the needle, boil the ocean, operationalize, incentivize, socialize (metaphorical), robust, scalable, holistic, ecosystem (outside biology), paradigm.

**When jargon is OK:** Technical terms of art with a precise shared meaning for the audience ("API rate limit" in dev docs; legal/medical terms for that field).
**When it's not:** Customer-facing copy; anywhere a plain word carries the same meaning; when it's there to sound important.

**Violation:** "We need to leverage our core competencies to ideate scalable solutions that move the needle on key stakeholder KPIs."
**Fix:** "We need to use what we're good at to come up with ideas that matter to our customers."

## Rule 4 — Verify quotations ("Check your quotations.")

**The test:** Flag every quotation and stat for a re-check before publishing.

**Violation patterns:** attributed quotes ("As Einstein said...") — misattributed most of the time; paraphrases dressed as quotations; quotes with no source; round numbers presented as precise.

**How to handle:** Don't assume the quote is wrong — re-check it. Ogilvy's rule is simply "check your quotations." If there are none, this rule passes.

## Rule 5 — Read-aloud test ("Read it aloud the next morning — and then edit it.")

**The test:** Read each sentence aloud in your head. Does it stumble? Tongue-twisters, awkward rhythm, repeated sounds, or a spot where you run out of breath?

**Violation patterns:**
- Strings of similar sounds: "The situation's specification specifies..."
- Long sentences with no comma rest (35 words, one breath).
- Unintentional rhymes: "The initiation of the presentation caused frustration..."
- Accidental word repeats: "We will review the review of the review process."

The "sleep on it" half of the rule is behavioral — you can't automate it, but you can always read the draft aloud once before it goes out.

**Violation:** "The comprehensive implementation of the recommendation requires examination of the documentation."
**Fix:** "To act on the recommendation, we'll need to review the docs."

## Rule 6 — Clear CTA ("Make sure it is crystal clear what you want the recipient to do.")

**The test:** From the writing alone, can you state in one sentence what the reader should do next? If you have to guess, it fails.

**Violation patterns:** information dumps with no ask; asks buried mid-paragraph; vague requests ("let me know your thoughts" — by when? for what decision?); passive asks ("it would be great if someone could..."); FYI-framed messages that are actually requests.

**When no CTA is needed:** Pure updates — but say so: "No action needed, sharing for awareness."

**Violation:** "...wanted to share some thoughts on the Q3 roadmap. There are a few tradeoffs worth considering... I think we should probably decide by Friday. Let me know."
**Fix:** "I need a decision by Friday: prioritize feature A or feature B in Q3. Reply with your pick and any concerns. Context below."

## Rule 7 — Comprehension (R&R: "People seldom act on what they cannot understand.")

**The test:** Could the actual recipient — not an insider — understand it on first read?

**Violation patterns:** unexplained acronyms (especially internal); references to context the reader may not share ("as discussed at the offsite"); compressed logic that skips steps; pronouns with no clear antecedent ("they said this would happen" — who?); assumed knowledge ("obviously, given our Q2 pivot...").

This is broader than jargon (Rule 3): after reading the whole thing, does the reader actually know what's going on? **Diagnostic:** hand it to someone who wasn't in the last meeting — do they get it?

**Violation:** "Given the PM's view on the KR slippage and what Jake flagged yesterday, we should escalate to the ELT before the ops review."
**Fix:** "Jake flagged yesterday that we'll miss our Q3 goal on customer response time. Our PM agrees it's serious. Can we get this to the leadership team before Thursday's ops review?"

---

## Rule 8 — Completeness (R&R: "When you say something, make sure you have said it.")

**The test:** Did you actually state the thing, or dance around it?

**Violation patterns:** questions implied but not asked; requests implied but not made; conclusions implied but not stated; the main point arriving halfway through or not at all; subject lines that hint rather than declare.

**Diagnostic:** What is the ONE thing the reader must take away? Is that thing written down, in plain language, where they'll see it? Broader than the CTA rule: a message can need no action and still fail by never stating its point.

**Violation:** "I was thinking about the timeline and some dependencies, and Sarah mentioned concerns last week. There's a lot going on with vendors too. Anyway, just some things on my mind."
**Fix:** "I think we're going to miss the November launch. Sarah and I have each flagged concerns, and vendor availability is a new risk. I want to talk through pushing to December."

## Rule 9 — Honesty / no hedging (R&R: "Bad news is not made better by being baffling as well as unwelcome.")

**The test:** Are you saying what you mean, or hiding behind softening language? Applies with extra force to bad news.

**Euphemisms:** "right-sizing" → layoffs; "part ways" → fired; "challenging" → failing; "opportunity for improvement" → problem; "go in a different direction" → we're rejecting your proposal.
**Weasel words:** "arguably," "somewhat," "it could be argued," "some might say" (when you hold the view); "we believe" as a shield; stacked hedges ("potentially," "possibly," "likely"); passive that hides the actor ("mistakes were made").
**Bad news specifically:** burying the lede in sympathy language; "after careful consideration..." openings that delay the point; explaining the process instead of stating the outcome.

**When hedging is OK:** genuine, acknowledged uncertainty ("I'm not sure — could be A or B"); calibrated confidence ("likely" when it's really ~70%).

**Violation:** "After careful consideration of a number of factors... we have made the difficult decision to pursue a restructuring that will unfortunately impact approximately 15% of the workforce."
**Fix:** "We're laying off 15% of the company. Here's why, and here's what happens next."

---

## Rule 10 — Facts with context (R&R: "Never present facts on their own, like unstrung gems.")

**The test:** For each fact, number, or data point: does the reader know why it matters? Is it strung into a point?

**Violation patterns:** stats with no interpretation ("Revenue was $4.2M."); bullet lists of numbers with no argument; "the data shows..." with no "...therefore..."; percentages without denominators ("engagement up 40%" — from what, over what period, vs. what benchmark?); cherry-picked metrics with no sense of normal.

**The metaphor:** Facts are building materials. Nobody is impressed by a pile of bricks. Show the building. **Diagnostic:** for every fact, ask "so what?" If the text doesn't answer, the fact is unstrung.

**Violation:** "Q3 revenue was $4.2M. CAC was $340. NPS was 62. Churn was 3.1%."
**Fix:** "Q3 was our best quarter yet. Revenue hit $4.2M (up 30% YoY), and we pulled CAC down to $340 while keeping NPS above 60. Churn ticked up to 3.1% — that's the one number to watch."

## Judgment & calibration

The rules are tools, not commandments. A great piece can violate a rule if the violation serves it. Your job is to notice when a rule is being broken *without serving the writing*, and fix that.

**Severity, when you self-audit:** ask "would a sharp, time-pressed reader notice, and think worse of it?"
- Yes, and they'd stop reading → critical, fix it.
- Yes, and they'd frown → moderate, fix it unless the register argues otherwise.
- Only if they were looking for it → minor, optional.

**When the piece breaks many rules at once,** find the pattern instead of listing every instance. Three jargon examples make the point.

**When it's clean, leave it clean.** Don't manufacture problems, and don't pad — an audit or edit that violates Ogilvy's own brevity is embarrassing. If a fix is one word, use one word.
