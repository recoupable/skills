## Modules and their different tests

| Module | Funding treatment | Evidence of success |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | Protected up-front commitment | Actual reuse and adoption; comparable deployment lead time, cost, security-review time, reliability and safety |
| Experimentation | Protected, affordable-to-lose learning budget | Useful scale/revise/stop decisions from capped tests on real work |
| Efficiency | Earn further funding with function-specific evidence | Captured economic value from capacity/cost improvements, net of full costs |
| Innovation | Earn further funding with function-specific evidence | Attributable incremental business growth evaluated with margins and costs |

The framework has four spending categories: foundation plus three budgets. It does not require equal allocations or demand immediate financial ROI from shared enabling work and learning. Foundation and experiments still need accountable outcomes. IRR/NPV alone miss those outcomes. Evidence strength, opportunity size and leadership judgment guide funding after each function's own gate is met.

## Source defaults and exact denominators

For early AI programs the author suggests an AI ceiling around 0.5–2% of annual revenue. Foundation is 8–12% of that AI ceiling, with the prose qualifying this range for regulated settings such as insurance. The spending image assigns experiments 10–20% of the amount remaining AFTER foundation. Efficiency and innovation share the residual pool; neither has a mandatory separate percentage. These are starting ranges to adapt to cash position, risk, maturity and goals, not approved spending or universal industry benchmarks.

Let R denote annual revenue, a denote the chosen ceiling rate, f denote the foundation share, and e denote the post-foundation experiment share:

- AI ceiling B = R × a
- Foundation F = B × f
- Experiments E = (B − F) × e
- Earned pool P = B − F − E

Example using synthetic figures: R = 100 million, a = 1%, f = 10%, e = 15%. B = 1 million, F = 100,000, E = 135,000 and P = 765,000. Existing contractual commitments reduce headroom; reconcile them into those amounts. Do not add the existing commitments on top of a supposedly all-in ceiling.

The author suggests reinvesting about 20% of realized gains into early foundation, tapering as it matures. Treat this as a proposal subject to approval and an explicit gross/net basis, timing and ceiling reconciliation. Never recycle forecast gains, count reinvestment as a second return, or silently exceed the ceiling.

## Nuances preserved from prose and figures

- A passing pilot can justify investment before there are realized financial returns. Match the evidence required to investment size and risk. Failed subgroups remain excluded or get a specific revised test.
- Efficiency capture has four routes: genuine avoided hiring, throughput replacing paid work or economically costly backlog, productive redeployment with demonstrated outcomes, and reduced expenditure. Spare hours alone prove none of them.
- Foundation shares data definitions, access/integrations and security work. Build it with the first two or three use cases, guided by valuable work blocked on missing capabilities. Measure actual use of training and tools, not just availability.
- Foundation's contribution is enablement, not a second claim on an agent's savings. Comparisons between different deployments require reuse evidence and adjustments for scope, complexity and reliability.
- Review efficiency provisional claims after one to two quarters and remove those that fail to appear. Innovation may need two to four quarters; leading evidence can support a decision within one quarter.
- The innovation image distinguishes observed lift from AI-attributable lift using a matched contemporaneous control. A forecast of all-company opportunity must not be booked as benefit from a half-company test.
- The efficiency figure labels a monetary net result as ROI; this skill reports net benefit in currency and ROI separately as a ratio. The quarterly figure distinguishes a negative six-month result from a positive forecast for the full year. Preserve both rather than calling the forecast realized.
- A pilot charged to experimentation is outside the article's rollout-only illustration but still belongs in total program spending and economics. Separate accounting views and reconcile them.
- Departmental metrics remain the starting point. Faster pull requests, happy users, content volume and estimated hours alone are insufficient proof of business value.
- The portfolio review asks whether money is captured, pilots conclude, and shared capabilities improve delivery. A large collection of successful demos can still be an unsuccessful program.
