## Design the smallest complete offer

**Source: chapter 2, pp. 61–82.** Snyder treats supply as a plan for completing a customer's project. His minimum viable supply can be software, a service, or a combination. A smaller feature set is not necessarily a smaller burden for the buyer: installation, migration, decisions, and operating the tool may create another project they do not want.

For the existing real-person PULL case:

1. Name the completed outcome and the buyer's actual alternatives.
2. Describe a delivery plan: what we do, what the buyer must do, timing, dependencies, and price. Mark proposed or unbuilt capabilities honestly.
3. **Check comprehension.** Can the buyer explain what the offer is, how it completes the project, how it differs from the options they considered, and what it costs? Pause after the short explanation to find misunderstandings.
4. **Check preference.** Given that understanding, does the buyer prefer this plan? Investigate reasons such as effort, uncertainty, control, expertise, timing, and price; do not assume everyone wants the cheapest or fastest tool.
5. Test the smallest delivery arrangement that can achieve the outcome. A manually produced result may fit better than selling the buyer another system to operate. Record provider labor and capacity so a successful service is not mistaken for scalable software economics.

If demand is grounded but the delivery form is uncertain, compare concrete offers for that same project. If the projects themselves differ, keep separate cases and default to sequential learning. Chapter 4 (pp. 125–140) warns that simultaneous demand stories multiply acquisition, sales, and delivery complexity. A single-person starting point still needs replication; it does not justify indefinitely building whatever that person requests.

## Diagnose the point where progress stops

**Sources: chapter 5, pp. 151–170; chapter 6, pp. 173–193; chapter 7, pp. 208–211.** Use the buyer's words, actions, and surrounding context. The categories below are an operational decision aid, not a score or a claim that every objection has one cause.

| Suspected gap | Evidence to distinguish it | Useful next move |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | The project is absent, deferred, or adequately served by existing options. | Clarify actual current work; reconsider whom or when to approach. Capture a different project as a new case. |
| Comprehension | The buyer cannot explain the offer or places it in a category that does not describe what is actually delivered. | Inspect the wording, example, or demo that caused confusion; test a clearer, truthful description. |
| Fit | The buyer understands the offer but identifies unacceptable effort, risk, scope, economics, or timing. | Investigate that specific trade-off. Change delivery, target situation, or offer only as the evidence supports. |
| Buying process | Demand and preference are supported, but a named approval, verification, or implementation requirement remains. | Work with the buyer on owners, dates, and required evidence. Use the field guide's pilot gate when relevant. |
| Delivery | A customer bought but has not achieved the promised value. | Check changed/misunderstood demand, expectation mismatch, and execution failure separately; repair the relevant promise, selection criterion, or delivery step. |

Do not diagnose a price problem merely because a buyer hesitates. Concern about whether the outcome will happen may be a risk problem; concern about implementation may be a fit problem. The book's guarantee example illustrates diagnosis, not a recommendation to offer guarantees by default. Any proposed commercial change needs credible delivery economics and the applicable authority.

In the conversation, invite disagreement about the situation and offer. Ask which part does not fit, follow the answer, and avoid continuing a demo after discovering that its premise is wrong. Record both the objection and what immediately preceded it. Treat a revised interpretation as a hypothesis until subsequent behavior supports it. Reframing an offer must not conceal migration, labor, limitations, or risk that remains real.

Separate agreement that the offer fits from coordinating the purchase. A smooth procurement checklist cannot establish missing demand, and an unresolved approval does not by itself disprove demand.

## Compare successful and unsuccessful cases

**Sources: chapter 3, pp. 105–121; chapter 6, pp. 179–188.** Start the candidate success pattern from one customer who both wanted to buy and achieved the promised outcome. Compare that person's situation with nonbuyers, stalled buyers, and disappointed customers. This directs discovery toward a concrete contrast rather than an average persona assembled from conflicting needs.

Compare project, trigger, prior setup, alternatives, perceived limitations, offer, buyer effort, and achieved result. Keep each person's evidence intact. A pattern such as “buyers with an existing system stall over migration; buyers starting fresh succeed” is a candidate selection rule to retest, not proof of causation or a reason to erase the stalled cases.

Maintain the existing evidence stages: a grounded anecdote, a paid customer, demonstrated value, repeatable success, and an economical acquisition/delivery process answer different questions. Do not turn the book's illustrative customer counts into universal graduation thresholds. Pair strong individual cases with the full cohort counts and observation periods before claiming repeatability.

**When alternatives or retention change (chapter 2, pp. 88–90):** investigate direct substitutes, a better interpretation of the same project, an upstream solution that removes the need, and the next project that makes the current solution obsolete. These are questions to investigate, not presumed reasons for churn. Successful completion of a one-off project is still successful completion.

## Improve the constraint on customer outcomes

**Source: chapter 7, pp. 195–223.** Pipeline, sales, and delivery form one system. Its useful output is customers receiving the promised outcome, with continued value appropriate to the business. Meeting volume or closed deals alone can conceal failure later in the process.

For a review:

1. Use comparable counts and time windows across conversations, fitting demand, purchases, delivered outcomes, and continued value. Account for sales/delivery lag and incomplete observation periods.
2. Compare feasible interventions by the additional successful outcomes they could produce, their effort/cost, and downstream capacity. Do not automatically optimize the smallest percentage. A small improvement in one rate may matter less than relieving delivery capacity or finding buyers with the right project.
3. Label estimates and dependencies. Sparse or mismatched data calls for a measurement step, not precise projections. Early learning access may be expensive; track it separately from evidence of economical acquisition.
4. Review actual call or delivery records together and choose one concrete change or bounded test, with an owner and checkpoint. The book suggests frequent reviews, including after roughly five calls early on; choose a cadence appropriate to the buying cycle and available evidence.
5. After changing the target buyer or project, reassess the offer, channel, sales process, and delivery assumptions that relied on the old case.

Before adding people or automation, check incentives across the process. A meeting target can reward irrelevant prospects; a sales target can reward promises delivery cannot fulfill. Keep teams connected to customer evidence and evaluate local improvements against delivered outcomes, retention, and economics. This adapts the book's discussion of purpose drift; it is not a ban on targets or specialization.

Chapters 8–9 (pp. 227–255) reinforce learning from present customer behavior and concentrating effort on what currently matters. Apply this by ending with a next action that can resolve uncertainty. Keep long-range plans revisable; additional analysis should serve a decision rather than postpone contact with customers. Do not interpret the argument as permission to ignore financial constraints, commitments, or wellbeing.
