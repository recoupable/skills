## Discovery and the two-slide test

Use these prompts to uncover the buyer's situation, not to lead them toward a preferred answer. Adapt wording to the person and their work.

1. **Opening:** What prompted you to take this call? Reflect their answer into an agreed agenda, then ask permission to explore their context.
2. **Project:** What are you trying to accomplish in this workflow that you cannot currently get done? Ask for a recent concrete attempt. “Adopt AI” is not yet a project; identify what AI is supposed to accomplish.
3. **Unavoidable:** Why now? What happens if you leave this as it is? A deadline in the future can still create action today when necessary preparation starts now. Do not invent pressure.
4. **Options:** What have you tried or considered? Could your team, current tools, a provider, or doing nothing get you through?
5. **Limitations:** Where exactly does each option fail? If it works well enough, let that answer disconfirm the bet. A theoretically better product does not make an unblocked person a buyer.
6. **Reflect:** Restate the four fields briefly and ask what is wrong or missing. Do not fill the missing urgency yourself.
7. **Offer:** Describe only the help that removes the established blocker. Ask what they would need to move forward and observe whether they initiate a concrete purchase step.

### Two-slide conversation aid

These are text outlines, not a request to render a presentation.

**Slide 1 — Their situation:**

“You are trying to [project]. It matters now because [reason]. You have considered [options], but [specific limitations] prevent the outcome. Have I understood that correctly?”

**Slide 2 — Proposed help:**

“We could help you achieve [outcome] by [narrow approach], with [scope, delivery mode, timing, and price hypothesis]. [Existing capability] is available; [manual work/unbuilt capability] would be part of delivery. What would you need to decide whether to start?”

The second slide is a testable offer, not a product vision. When capabilities or pricing are unknown, label them as proposals to validate. Do not promise a finished platform if the first version is a service.

### Hail Mary

When the proposed project does not matter enough, ask what work on their plate they would pay someone to complete now. Capture the answer as a **different hypothesis** with its own PULL fields; do not quietly count it as validation of the original idea.

### Getting the conversation

Use a brief, truthful, audience-relevant reason to meet. Founder history or firsthand domain experience can be useful; the message's job is to start a conversation, not to prove the whole value proposition. Do not invent credentials, investor names, mutual contacts, or personalization. Draft within the authorized channel; sending needs existing authority. The source suggests aiming for two to three conversations per day once access works, not an automatic quota or permission to spam.

## Signals, pricing, and buying process

Keep evidence stages separate; this is a qualitative reading guide, not a scoring rubric.

| Observation | Interpretation | Next evidence |
|---|---|---|
| “Interesting,” feature suggestions, hypothetical future use | Interest only; pull unproven | Current project, why now, real alternatives, willingness to act |
| Buyer supplies all four PULL fields but has taken no buying step | Plausible pull hypothesis | Test a concrete, credible offer |
| Buyer asks how to start, requests a contract, or initiates procurement | Buying behavior; not automatically a completed sale | Named owner, next step/date, commercial decision |
| Money paid or a binding order, as supported by the record | Sale supported | Fulfill the promised outcome and observe retention |
| Customer reaches a meaningful value event | Delivery evidence | Continued use/renewal over a relevant period |
| Comparable buyers buy, receive the same outcome, and stay | Evidence of a repeatable pattern | Test the current pipeline/sales/delivery constraint and economics |

Body language can prompt a follow-up but cannot replace behavior. Silence or missing call notes is not a negative verdict. A champion's pull is not proof that every approver agrees; identify who uses, buys, funds, and approves the work.

### Price without fictional anchors

Start with the value of completing the project and the buyer's real cost of alternatives. Early on, willingness to transact matters more than perfect price optimization. Make the offer concrete enough that a buyer can decide; do not replace a purchase test with an abstract willingness-to-pay survey.

The episode describes discussing comparable pricing examples to invite a buyer's reaction. Use only real, sourced comparables or explicitly hypothetical ranges. Do not imply other customers paid a made-up amount. Track concessions and founder labor so a discounted or service-heavy sale is not mistaken for scalable economics.

### Enterprise and pilot nuance

Once a credible buyer/project and buying process are identified, ask when the customer needs the outcome working. Work backward through procurement, security, budget, legal, onboarding, and delivery as applicable, with owners and dates. Procurement activity is evidence of progress, not closed revenue; no movement calls for investigation, not automatic dismissal or automatic validation.

Do not proactively offer free design partnerships as a substitute for selling an outcome. If the customer's actual buying process requires a pilot, define scope, price or explicit commercial status, success measures, sponsor/decision owner, end date, and the decision that follows. A required pilot is not PMF by itself.

## Learning batches and recovery

Before testing, write down what result would change your mind. Select buyers with a comparable project and situation; do not blend unrelated segments to manufacture apparent agreement.

After roughly five conversations, review what changed. The episode explicitly treats this as a heuristic, not a universal sample size or pass mark:

- **Several buyers try to purchase the same outcome:** pursue the real next commercial step and begin delivery learning; keep testing repeatability.
- **One subgroup pulls:** specify what differs and test more buyers in that subgroup. One customer's demand may remain bespoke.
- **Polite interest, no buying behavior:** locate the missing P/U/L/L field, change the hypothesis or offer, and test again.
- **They can already get it done:** their alternatives are adequate. Do not treat “better UX” or “more AI” as sufficient rebuttals.
- **Different urgent work emerges:** record a new hypothesis from that work; retain the original result.
- **No useful learning:** go observe or participate in the workflow firsthand. Change access or method instead of repeating hundreds of undirected interviews.

The episode's “goblin mode” is an occasional intensive search for firsthand contact when stuck. Translate that into a bounded, user-authorized fieldwork sprint with a goal, time/budget limit, and review point. It is not a requirement to travel, spend, harass prospects, or overwork.

## Delivery and the case-study factory

**Candidate X:** Specify the earliest event that plausibly gives the customer the promised value, plus how soon it should happen. Do not borrow another product's numerical activation threshold. Observe whether X actually predicts retention for this product and segment. If the observation window is incomplete, leave retention pending.

For a one-off project, measure its promised completion outcome and repeat purchase/referral evidence when relevant; do not invent subscription renewal or treat a successfully finished job as churn. For recurring products, use a retention window appropriate to their actual usage cycle.

**Firsthand delivery:** Stay close to the first customers' work. Record where they stall before value, what manual intervention fixes it, and whether the same fix works for the next comparable customer. Honest service delivery can uncover the product; custom work alone does not prove a repeatable software business.

**Cohorts:** Compare retained and churned customers by buyer/project, trigger, alternatives, promised outcome, and time to value. Product usage can reveal a narrow repeatable workflow inside a broad offer. Keep counts, denominators, observation periods, and exceptions visible before recommending a focus change.

**Case-study factory:** The reusable unit is one recognizable customer success pattern, not a requirement to publish testimonials. Diagnose pipeline, sales, and delivery separately:

- Few conversations with the right buyers: test access and targeting.
- Enough comparable conversations, little purchase progress: revisit PULL/offer fit or the buying process using call evidence.
- Sales without value or retention: investigate delivery, the value event, and segment fit before pouring in more leads.

Work on the evidenced constraint and measure again. Do not force all problems into a single explanation when data is missing.
